# Writing the Six Accounts

[简体中文](overview.zh-CN.md) · [Shared writing principles](shared.md) · [Forming and revising working models](../design-and-revision.md)

The six accounts describe a particular collaboration between a person and a
model. Together they help both participants understand what they are doing,
make a fitting contribution, and correct what no longer works. Each account
answers a different question about the same practice.

| Account | Question for the writer |
| --- | --- |
| [Orientation](orientation.md) | What does this person want the collaboration to serve, and what reasons and standards guide that choice? |
| [Person](person.md) | How does this person actually participate, and under which conditions can they contribute, learn, or make a judgment? |
| [Counterpart](counterpart.md) | What can this model actually contribute through the setting in which the person encounters it, and what contribution do they want? |
| [Relationship](relationship.md) | How do the two contributions meet and change what the other participant can do? |
| [Environment](environment.md) | What real arrangements make those contributions possible, and where can the participants see the result? |
| [Revision](revision.md) | What new grounds or consequences can change the current understanding, the arrangement, or the method of revising it? |

## Begin with a real activity

Choose a piece of work whose purpose, participation, and result you can inspect.
An unfinished thought, a difficult handoff, or a successful way of returning to
a draft can supply a starting point. Gather the person's account of it, what
the model contributed, and what happened under the actual conditions. Bring in
philosophical material that explains a distinction or supports a chosen direction.
Yinyang offers one worked example of these connections. A recipient may adopt
some of its concepts or develop a different account.

Write where the material gives you most to say. A difficulty with a tool can
open the environment question and then change the account of participation.
An insight about learning can start with orientation and then change the
relationship. Return to the table to check which other question a new claim
affects. In a complete set, each account can be read for its own question and
the six can be used together.

## Keep each contribution with its question

Consider someone learning to read difficult essays. Orientation can explain
why learning to evaluate an argument matters to them. Person can show why a
well-placed question helps them express an initial reading. Counterpart can
describe whether the model can cite the exact passage and formulate a serious
objection. Relationship can explain who offers the first interpretation and
how feedback changes the next attempt. Environment can describe how the
passage, annotation, and discussion stay findable. Revision can identify what
evidence would justify changing that arrangement and how the change could be checked.
One activity gives six connected answers without requiring six retellings.

Use the [shared principles](shared.md) for evidence, condensation, and checks
across the whole. Use the guide for each question to write its distinctive
contribution. Keep fuller sources reachable in the practice's own materials.
When the work changes, the [revision method](revision-method.md) gives ways to
carry a supported correction through the affected accounts and actual use.
